# The Delhi-Berlin Compact

This is a new, separate-command German route for a fresh Indian campaign. It is not formal Axis membership. Signing it starts no war and does not make Germany responsible for Indian peace settlements.

## How it begins

From 1937, choose a German or Axis-facing foreign policy and open **The Delhi-Berlin Strategic Conference**. The proposal costs 350 money and 600 supplies.

Berlin measures two existing achievements:

- a German industrial mission;
- an Indian mobile-army programme.

Both give the best odds for an equal compact. One gives a credible middle tier. Neither leaves India likely to receive a limited technical offer. A refusal is not permanent: the channel can reopen after six months.

## What signing does

The equal and limited versions both:

- set Germany as India's sole binding strategic commitment;
- retain Indian command;
- create no engine alliance;
- declare no war;
- block incompatible alliance compacts;
- suppress the older 1940 Delhi-Berlin conference, avoiding two competing German entry systems.

Formal Axis entry remains available later behind a second, explicit warning. Confirming it inherits Germany's live wars.

## Building an equal partnership

A limited compact can become equal after any two useful proofs:

- **Factories Without Masters:** machine-tool research or synthetic fuel.
- **Quetta-Berlin Staff Exchange:** land organisation or transport capacity.
- **Cape Raider School:** submarine morale or maritime research.
- **The Indian Legion Comes Home:** manpower with political cost, an officer cadre, or refusal.
- **A Seat, Not a Salute:** confront German racial hierarchy, protest privately, or tolerate it for access.

The options deliberately improve different weaknesses. No project gives free IC or a free fleet. If Berlin delays equality until battle, Indian control of Baku, Tashkent, Suez or Singapore forces the amendment.

## Choosing wars

The compact never automatically follows Germany into war. Separate persistent decisions allow India to open:

- war with Britain from 1939;
- war with the Soviet Union from 1940, whether or not Germany is already fighting it;
- war with Japan from 1940;
- war with the United States from 1941, after a German-American war exists.

Each docket has a no-effect Cancel option and declares war on one country only. Germany then recognizes, aids, pressures or condemns India's choice. German pressure cannot end India's existing war.

When Germany starts its own British, Soviet or American war, a target-specific crisis appears. Handling one crisis does not close the other two.

## Campaign chapters

The compact adds story beats for:

- Baku plus the Caucasus gate;
- a deep Baku-Tashkent-Soviet campaign;
- Aden and Suez;
- the Malacca sea gate;
- a combined Caucasus-Red Sea-Malacca command.

These are modest operational rewards and standing. They do not transfer provinces, create puppets or sign peace. The existing German, regional and national settlement systems remain authoritative, so victories are not paid twice.

## Politics and endings

Pre-war disputes with Tokyo and Rome define whether India accepts consultation, claims an Asian veto, rejects European ocean spheres or confines Berlin to continental cooperation.

If Germany collapses, India can send one finite lifeline, save its own fronts or become the sovereign successor. None of these choices forces peace.

From 1943, a successful India may reject Berlin's postwar limits. A compact-only India can eventually break with Germany, declare a separate war and record a dedicated victory after taking Berlin and another major European capital.

At peace, **Withdraw from the Delhi-Berlin Compact** ends the commitment after an explicit choice and opens foreign policy again after 90 days. Historical achievements remain recorded.
